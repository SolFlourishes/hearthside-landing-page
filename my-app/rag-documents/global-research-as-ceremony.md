## 💡 Core Thesis: Research Is Ceremony

The book *Research Is Ceremony: Indigenous Research Methods* (2008) by **Shawn Wilson** articulates the shared paradigm of research conducted by Indigenous scholars in Australia and Canada.

The work is written in two styles: an academic style and a personal narrative style, the latter of which is used to **build a relationship** between the storyteller and the readers, which is necessary for understanding the Indigenous paradigm.

> The core thesis is that **research is a ceremony**. The purpose of any ceremony is to **build stronger relationships** or bridge the distance between aspects of our cosmos and ourselves. Indigenous research is a ceremony that provides a **raised level of consciousness and insight**.

## ⭕ The Indigenous Research Paradigm (The Circle)

The Indigenous research paradigm is composed of four inseparable entities, often conceptualized in a circle. The entire paradigm is summarized as being **relational and maintaining relational accountability**.

### Four Entities of the Paradigm

**Ontology (What is real?)**
- **Relationality**: Reality is **relationships** or sets of relationships; objects themselves are not the primary focus.

**Epistemology (How do we know reality?)**
- **Relationality**: **Knowledge is relational** and shared with all of creation (cosmos, animals, plants, earth); it is systems of knowledge in their context.
- Ontology and epistemology are equivalent.

**Axiology (Ethics and Morals)**
- **Relational Accountability**: Value is based on fulfilling a **role and obligations** in the research relationship.
- **Respect, Reciprocity, and Responsibility** (the 3 R's) are key features.

**Methodology (How do we gain knowledge?)**
- **Relational Accountability**: The methodology is the process of **building more relations**.
- It must adhere to the ethic of respect and must be useful to the Indigenous community.

## 🔑 Key Philosophical Concepts

### Relationality and Identity
- **Self-as-Relationship:** Indigenous identity is grounded in relationships with the land, ancestors, and future generations; the self is **the relationships one holds**.
- **Cosmic Connections:** The relationships include the environment, land, cosmos, and ideas.
- **Spiritual Basis:** The space and relationship between people and their environment are sacred. Research as a ceremony is the **spiritual part**; the spiritual is an integral, infused part of the Indigenous worldview.

### Logic, Knowledge, and Discourse
- **Logic:** Indigenous methodology requires **intuitive logic** or circular thinking, as opposed to western linear logic. Intuitive logic looks at the entire system of relationships as a whole (synthesis).
- **Synthesis vs. Deconstruction:** Indigenous thinking starts with **synthesis** (building relationships) rather than deconstruction (breaking ideas down). Breaking things down into small parts destroys the relationships around them.
- **Knowledge Ownership:** Knowledge is **not individual** in nature, but belongs to the cosmos/collective. Gaining knowledge is like being married to an idea, meaning one shares a special, accountable relationship with it, rather than owning it.
- **Discourse Patterns:** The discourse style is circular, egalitarian, and non-interfering. The storyteller's role is to share information, not to draw conclusions for the listener, who must internalize the information and form their own conclusions.

### Critique of Dominant Paradigms
- **Objectivity:** The expectation of objectivity is dangerous, as emotionless research is a "goddamn lie". If a researcher pretends to be objective, they become dangerous, first to themselves and then to the people around them.
- **Ethical Concerns:** Research done by "outsiders" has been colonial and oppressive, often focusing on negative aspects (illness/problems) rather than community resources (health).
- **Validity:** Indigenous research rejects external measures like validity and reliability, seeking **authenticity** or **credibility** instead, which means the research accurately reflects and builds upon relationships.

## 🛠️ Applying Relational Accountability (The Practice)

The shared aspects of the Indigenous paradigm are put into practice through the choice of topic, methods, analysis, and presentation.

### Topic Choice
- Must be led by the community and result in positive change.
- Focusing energy on **harmony** and collective well-being rather than on ill-health.

### Methods/Data Collection
- Must build relationships (the methodology is building more relations).
- Must be **extra-intellectual**; reliance on intuition, dreams, or inspiration is included alongside empirical data.
- Techniques like **talking circles, talk story**, and participatory action research (PAR) fit well.

### Analysis
- Must be **collaborative, cumulative, and ongoing**.
- Uses **intuitive logic**; analysis must look at an entire system of relationships as a whole (synthesis).
- Continuous feedback with participants ensures accuracy (hermeneutic).

### Presentation
- Must honor relationships; knowledge is transferred through cultural context.
- Use of metaphor and symbolism allows the audience to form a relationship with the idea.
- The author must demonstrate humility and be willing to admit ignorance.
- Using the names of co-researchers and Elders honors those relationships.

## 📈 Progression of Indigenous Research

Indigenous scholars have mapped the development of Indigenous research, moving from justifying methodologies to articulating their own paradigm.

### Historical Phases

**Terra Nullius Phase (1770–1900)**
- Research consisted mainly of observations by Europeans.
- Indigenous people were viewed as impediments to progress.

**Assimilationist Phase (1940–1970)**
- Non-Aboriginal people became "experts," and the Indigenous voice was silenced.
- Research was used to justify policies like residential schools.

**First Stage (Early assimilationist)**
- Indigenous scholars situated themselves solidly in a Western framework, striving to mimic Western researchers.
- Scholars sought to separate their Indigenous lives from academic endeavors.

**Third Stage (Decolonization Focus)**
- Scholars challenged Western methods and began a process of Indigenizing methodologies.

**Fourth/Indigenist Research Phase (Present)**
- Indigenous scholars are challenging others to adopt an Indigenous research paradigm based on their own worldviews and ethical protocols.

## 💬 Communication Implications

### Relational Communication Patterns
- **Circular discourse:** Communication follows circular patterns rather than linear arguments
- **Non-interference:** Speakers share without imposing conclusions on listeners
- **Storytelling:** Knowledge is conveyed through narrative and metaphor
- **Collective knowledge:** Information belongs to the community, not individuals
- **Synthesis-first:** Building connections before breaking down details

### Western vs. Indigenous Communication
- **Western:** Linear logic, objective stance, individual ownership, deconstructive analysis
- **Indigenous:** Circular logic, relational stance, collective ownership, synthetic understanding

### Building Cross-Cultural Understanding
- Recognize that Indigenous communication prioritizes **relationship-building** over information exchange
- Understand that silence and reflection are valued communication practices
- Respect that knowledge sharing follows protocols of reciprocity and responsibility
- Acknowledge that emotional and spiritual dimensions are integral, not separate from "objective" communication
