## 💡 Core Concept: Accepting Boundaries> [cite_start]**Accepting Boundaries** is a key behavioral factor, alongside **effort** and **avoidance** (e.g., avoiding stigmatizing), required to **provide a barrier-free communicational situation**[cite: 1821, 1823].[cite_start]The guarantee of barrier-free communication relies on a social interaction where **various perspectives are considered and personal boundaries are accepted**[cite: 1824]. [cite_start]This mutual acceptance ensures that individuals who are neurologically divergent are not constantly interpreted based on neurotypical definitions of social behavior[cite: 1841].---## 🤝 Mechanism of Acceptance and Reciprocity[cite_start]Achieving barrier-free communication requires interlocutors to invest **well-balanced effort** proportional to their own limits for mutual understanding[cite: 1825].### Reciprocity Requirement[cite_start]**Reciprocity** is necessary for successful communication[cite: 1828].* [cite_start]Both non-autistic and autistic individuals must **accept and understand each other’s difficulties** and limitations, and help each other overcome them[cite: 1826, 1827].* [cite_start]Treating the other person as an equal partner means **taking the other person’s needs into account and adapting to them**[cite: 1828].### Attitude of Acceptance[cite_start]Beyond concrete strategies, participants must cultivate **patience and a kind attitude**[cite: 1829]. [cite_start]A failure of communication is often caused by a **negative attitude** toward the other person, not just a difference of opinion[cite: 1830].---## 🧠 Specific Adaptations and Boundaries### Autistic Boundaries* [cite_start]Autistic communication characteristics, such as **avoiding eye contact**, should not be taken personally or viewed as offensive or rude by the non-autistic partner[cite: 1831].* [cite_start]Disclosure of one's diagnosis could be beneficial for barrier-free communication, though the **fear of stigmatization** can make this decision challenging[cite: 1832].### Non-Autistic and Professional Adaptations* [cite_start]Professionals must **accept their *own* difficulty in interpreting** autistic behavior and intentions[cite: 1833].* [cite_start]They should **avoid communicating with similes, slurs, and sarcasm**, as these forms of language may not be understood by autistic individuals[cite: 1834].* [cite_start]They should **use communication aids** (like little drawings or flowcharts)[cite: 1835].* [cite_start]They must **not speak to autistic adults as if they were a child** or unequal[cite: 1835].* [cite_start]**Knowledge of autism**—and particularly the communication style of the **specific autistic interlocutor**—is necessary for the non-autistic person to select the appropriate communication tool[cite: 1836].---## 🌐 Broader Context: Proxemics (Personal Space)In nonverbal communication, boundaries are crucial. [cite_start]People regulate their proxemic (space) and haptic (touch) acts to maintain **privacy boundaries**[cite: 1837].* [cite_start]**Personal space** is conceptualized as a pervasive portable bubble or **protective sphere** that helps regulate contact in social situations[cite: 1838].* [cite_start]Intrusion into one’s personal space **generates reactions of discomfort or flight**[cite: 1839].* [cite_start]Privacy and boundaries must be upheld through adjustments, whether physical (markers) or nonverbal (avoiding eye contact in crowded situations), to discourage interaction[cite: 1840].
